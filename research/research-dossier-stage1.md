## AI Ethics, Alignment & Safety

This section surveys the literature that the project's founding hypothesis must sit against: how the field currently defines and operationalizes "AI ethics," "alignment," "safety," "responsible AI," and "machine ethics," and where value pluralism and non-human interests already appear (or are conspicuously absent) in that literature. It is a literature review, not a design document.

### AI Ethics: definitions and major frameworks

There is no single agreed definition of "AI ethics." A systematic literature review covering 62 peer-reviewed frameworks published between 2015 and 2024 that integrate ethics into AI product development, and a separate PRISMA-based review of 22 papers, white papers and policy documents (2020-2025), both find broad convergence on a shortlist of principles — fairness, transparency, accountability, explainability, privacy, and sustainability — but no consensus on a formal definition of the field itself: out of 73 studies reviewed in one meta-analysis, only 7 offered an explicit definition of "AI ethics," and most works define the field ostensively, by listing principles rather than stating what the field is [SUPPORTED] (Frontiers in AI, 2025, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1619029/full; systematic review, ResearchGate/iJIM, 2025, https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jim/article/view/56981).

Philosophically, published frameworks typically ground these principles in one or more of the classical normative traditions — consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics — applied across the AI development lifecycle, and some frameworks import the four principles of biomedical ethics (beneficence, non-maleficence, respect for autonomy, justice) as a ready-made cross-domain ethical vocabulary [SUPPORTED] (Springer, "A review of ethical AI frameworks in product development," 2025, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-025-00952-w). This matters for the project's founding hypothesis: the dominant frameworks in the literature are largely anthropocentric by construction (fairness *to whom*, autonomy *of whom* are typically left implicitly human) — extending the same principle vocabulary to non-human sentient beings is not something the mainstream AI-ethics literature has systematically attempted [OPEN QUESTION].

### AI Alignment: value alignment, outer/inner alignment

"AI alignment" research asks how to make an AI system's actual behavior track the goals and values intended by its designers or by some broader normative standard. The field has a now-standard technical decomposition: **outer alignment** concerns whether the specified objective (e.g., a reward function or fine-tuning target) actually captures designers' intentions, while **inner alignment** concerns whether the model's learned, internally represented objective matches that specified objective after training — the risk being that a model optimizes a proxy objective that diverges from the specification under distribution shift ("goal misgeneralization") [ESTABLISHED, definitional consensus in the technical literature] (Ji et al., "AI Alignment: A Comprehensive Survey," arXiv:2310.19852, 2023, updated 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852; "AI Alignment: A Contemporary Survey," ACM Computing Surveys, 2025, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3770749; Shen et al., "Large Language Model Alignment: A Survey," arXiv:2309.15025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15025).

A foundational philosophical treatment by Iason Gabriel (then at DeepMind) separates the alignment problem into a technical question (how to reliably build systems that track a specification) and a normative question (which values or principles the system should track, given reasonable disagreement about morality among humans) — and argues that under a "fact of reasonable pluralism," alignment cannot simply presuppose a single correct moral theory to align to [SUPPORTED] (Gabriel, "Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment," *Minds and Machines*, 2020, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2). This is directly relevant to the project's premise: mainstream alignment research already recognizes that "align to human values" is underspecified because humans do not share one value system — the open question the project inherits is not whether to resolve that pluralism, but whether and how to widen the circle of whose values/interests are represented at all, beyond humans [OPEN QUESTION].

Practically, the dominant current alignment technique for large language models is preference-based fine-tuning: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and its AI-feedback variant, Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF), of which Anthropic's **Constitutional AI** is the most cited instance — a two-stage method in which a model critiques and revises its own outputs against a written set of natural-language principles, then is further trained via reinforcement learning using a preference model trained on AI-generated (not human-generated) comparisons [ESTABLISHED] (Bai et al., "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," arXiv:2212.08073, Anthropic, 2022; secondary discussion in "Constitution or Collapse? Exploring Constitutional AI with Llama 3-8B," arXiv:2504.04918, https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04918v1). Constitutional AI is notable for this project because it demonstrates, at production scale, that a written charter of principles — rather than only aggregated human preference labels — can steer model behavior; the open research question is whether such a charter mechanism could in principle encode consideration for non-human sentient beings' interests, and whether "AI feedback" evaluators can meaningfully represent interests no human labeler holds [OPEN QUESTION].

### AI Safety: core concepts and research agendas

"AI safety" is generally treated as a subfield of AI alignment/robustness research focused on preventing unintended, harmful behavior, with three canonical technical pillars proposed by DeepMind's safety research team: **specification** (does the stated objective match designer intent), **robustness** (does the system perform reliably outside its training distribution, including under distributional shift and adversarial pressure), and **assurance** (can we verify and monitor that a deployed system is behaving as intended, rather than merely hoping so) [SUPPORTED] (DeepMind Safety Research, "Building safe artificial intelligence: specification, robustness, and assurance," 2018, https://deepmindsafetyresearch.medium.com/building-safe-artificial-intelligence-specification-robustness-and-assurance-52f5f75058f1). An earlier, widely cited agenda-setting paper from the Future of Life Institute similarly frames "beneficial AI" research priorities around verification, validity, security, and control, alongside a call for research on the long-term, economic, and legal implications of advanced AI [SUPPORTED] (Russell, Dewey, Tegmark, "Research Priorities for Robust and Beneficial Artificial Intelligence," arXiv:1602.03506, 2015, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.03506).

Current AI safety scholarship remains almost entirely framed around harms *to humans* (misuse, loss of control, societal disruption); harms to non-human sentient beings caused by AI-driven systems (e.g., industrial agriculture optimization, ecosystem-scale automated decision-making, wildlife-affecting logistics) are not a recognized category in the mainstream safety literature reviewed here [OPEN QUESTION] — a gap directly relevant to whether "safety" as currently defined is a sufficient frame for the project's aims, or whether a distinct "welfare/harm-to-sentient-beings" evaluation axis needs to be proposed as a novel contribution [PROJECT PROPOSAL].

### Responsible AI and AI Governance

"Responsible AI" functions in the literature less as a distinct research program and more as an umbrella term for the practice of designing, developing, and deploying AI systems in ways consistent with fairness, transparency, accountability, and safety commitments, typically operationalized through the major intergovernmental and standards frameworks [SUPPORTED]. The **OECD AI Principles** (2019, updated 2024) articulate five principles — inclusive growth/well-being, respect for human rights and democratic values including fairness and privacy, transparency and explainability, robustness/security/safety, and accountability — adopted as a reference point by over 40 countries, with the 2024 update adding guidance on generative AI content provenance and systemic risk monitoring [ESTABLISHED] (OECD, "OECD AI Principles," https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles). The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (2023) operationalizes similar commitments into four functions — govern, map, measure, manage — and explicitly draws on the OECD principles [ESTABLISHED] (NIST, "Trustworthy AI: Managing the Risks of Artificial Intelligence," https://www.nist.gov/speech-testimony/trustworthy-ai-managing-risks-artificial-intelligence).

**AI governance** as an academic field is defined by GovAI (formerly housed at Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute, which closed in April 2024; GovAI now operates as an independent research organization) as "the norms and institutions shaping how AI is built and deployed, as well as the policy and research efforts to make it go well" [SUPPORTED, note institutional history] (GovAI mission statement, cited via Oxford Handbook of AI Governance overview chapter, https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/41989/chapter-abstract/408516484; closure of FHI documented in LessWrong, "FHI has shut down (2005-2024)," https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/tu3CH22nFLLKouMKw/fhi-future-of-humanity-institute-has-shut-down-2005-2024, and Daily Nous, "The End of the Future of Humanity Institute," https://dailynous.com/2024/04/18/end-future-of-humanity-institute/). The *Oxford Handbook of AI Governance* organizes the field into value foundations, regulatory ecosystems, implementation frameworks, and international politics — again, its "value foundations" section is built around human political and economic values rather than non-human welfare [OPEN QUESTION] (Oxford University Press, https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/41989).

### Machine Ethics / Computational Ethics

Machine ethics (also called machine morality or computational morality) is a recognized subfield distinct from "computer ethics" (which studies human use of computing) — it studies how to design AI systems ("artificial moral agents," AMAs) that themselves behave in ethically acceptable ways, as opposed to studying how humans should use AI [ESTABLISHED] (JAIR, "Computational Machine Ethics: A Survey," https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/16836). A widely cited ACM Computing Surveys taxonomy classifies implementations along the ethical theory being operationalized (e.g., consequentialist, deontological, virtue-based, hybrid) and the technical means of implementation (rule-based systems, case-based reasoning, and increasingly learning-based/LLM approaches) [SUPPORTED] (Tolmeijer et al., "Implementations in Machine Ethics: A Survey," ACM Computing Surveys 53(6), 2020; arXiv:2001.07573, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.07573). A 2025 update notes a continuing shift toward learning-based and LLM-based artificial moral agency, alongside persistent open problems in evaluating whether such systems generalize moral judgments correctly outside their training scenarios [SUPPORTED] (Springer, "Trends and challenges in machine ethics," AI and Ethics, 2025, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-025-00971-7). Machine ethics is the closest existing academic subfield to what the project's technical work (an "ethical evaluation layer") would sit inside, and its taxonomy of ethical-theory-pluralism (a system can be "configurable" across ethical theories rather than committed to one) is directly reusable [SUPPORTED].

### Value Alignment vs. Value Pluralism: an unresolved tension

Recent alignment scholarship treats the tension between value alignment and value pluralism as a live, unresolved research problem rather than a solved design choice. One line of work argues that value pluralism is in tension with any single AI system's behavior being fully aligned to *everyone's* values simultaneously — because reasonable people hold conflicting values, "value pluralism is inconsistent with AI monism" [SUPPORTED] (arXiv:2404.19256, "AI, Pluralism, and (Social) Compensation," https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.19256). A NeurIPS 2024 paper (the COMAL framework) further distinguishes *first-order* value choices (which specific values/preferences a system implements) from *second-order* value choices (what makes the process of choosing those values legitimate), warning against "pluralistic value-washing," where a system claims pluralism cosmetically while remaining monistic in practice [SUPPORTED] (OpenReview, "Plurality of value pluralism and AI value alignment," https://openreview.net/forum?id=AOokh1UYLH). No consensus method yet exists for aggregating plural, potentially conflicting values — human or otherwise — into a single system's behavior in a way the field agrees is legitimate [OPEN QUESTION].

### Sentience, moral status, and AI: where the literature already goes non-anthropocentric

A small but active research strand — separate from mainstream alignment/safety — directly engages the project's founding hypothesis. Philosophical consensus in this literature ("philosophical orthodoxy") holds that sentience (the capacity for subjective/phenomenal experience, including suffering) is *sufficient* for moral patienthood, though not necessarily the *only* sufficient condition [SUPPORTED, contested at the margins] (80,000 Hours problem profile, "Understanding the moral status of digital minds," https://80000hours.org/problem-profiles/moral-status-digital-minds/). Applied to artificial systems specifically, a minority but notable position (associated with researchers such as Jeff Sebo and Robert Long) argues for a precautionary framework that takes seriously the non-negligible probability that some current or near-term AI systems could already possess morally relevant experience [SUPPORTED, explicitly contested] (arXiv:2501.07290, "Principles for Responsible AI Consciousness Research," https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.07290; arXiv:2502.00388, "The Societal Response to Potentially Sentient AI," https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00388).

Separately, and more directly relevant to the project's "non-human sentient beings" framing, a 2026 arXiv paper explicitly argues that anthropocentric definitions of AI-driven "flourishing" are becoming scientifically untenable as understanding of non-human animal sentience deepens, and that alignment work must explicitly weigh trade-offs between human prosperity and non-human animal welfare rather than treating human interests as automatically prioritized [HYPOTHESIS / PROJECT-ADJACENT, single-paper position, not yet a field consensus] (arXiv:2605.10310, "Positive Alignment: Artificial Intelligence for Human Flourishing," https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10310). This is the closest existing academic articulation found of the project's founding hypothesis; it should be treated as a precedent and interlocutor, not as validation [INTERPRETATION].

### Ethical Impact Assessment methodologies

The most developed operational methodology found is UNESCO's **Ethical Impact Assessment (EIA)**, part of the 2021 *Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence*: a practical, ex-ante and ex-post tool assessing whether a specific AI system aligns with the Recommendation's values, covering data quality/representativeness, team diversity, algorithmic robustness/transparency/auditability, and checkpointed review across the development lifecycle [ESTABLISHED] (UNESCO, https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethical-impact-assessment-tool-recommendation-ethics-artificial-intelligence; UNESCO Global AI Ethics and Governance Observatory, https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/eia). A systematic review of AI impact assessment methods more broadly finds the field modeled explicitly on environmental impact statements, but concludes it has not yet converged on agreed content, structure, or implementation standards [SUPPORTED] (PMC, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10037374/). No existing ethical impact assessment methodology found in this search explicitly scopes "affected entities" to include non-human sentient beings by default — all reviewed instruments define "impact" and "stakeholders" in human/organizational terms [OPEN QUESTION].

### Synthesis for the project's founding hypothesis

1. Mainstream alignment scholarship already accepts that "align AI to human values" is underspecified, because human values are plural and often conflicting [ESTABLISHED] — but virtually no work in the mainstream alignment, safety, responsible-AI, or ethical-impact-assessment literature extends the scope of "whose values/interests count" beyond humans by default [OPEN QUESTION]. The project's hypothesis is therefore not contradicted by the existing literature; it is largely orthogonal to it, occupying a scope the field has not yet systematically addressed.
2. A small number of recent position papers (2025-2026) make claims structurally similar to the project's hypothesis, but these are individual proposals, not consensus findings, and should be cited as precedent/interlocutor rather than as support [INTERPRETATION].
3. Existing machine-ethics taxonomies (ethical-theory-configurable systems) and existing moral-uncertainty/value-pluralism frameworks (first-order vs. second-order value choices) are directly reusable methodological building blocks for the project's later technical sections [SUPPORTED].

## Sentience & Moral Patienthood

### Sentience: definitions and usage

"Sentience" is used inconsistently across philosophy and science, which is itself a methodological problem for this project [OPEN QUESTION]. In the narrowest and most widely adopted technical sense, Jonathan Birch (LSE) defines sentience as "the capacity to have valenced experiences," i.e. experiences with a positive or negative quality such as pain, pleasure, or suffering (Birch, *The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals, and AI*, Oxford University Press, 2024, https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-edge-of-sentience-9780192870421) [ESTABLISHED as the definition Birch uses; SUPPORTED as a common convention in the animal-sentience literature]. A broader, looser everyday usage treats "sentience" as synonymous with "consciousness" or general awareness; this project adopts the narrower, valence-based definition because it is the one that does the ethical work — it links directly to interests that can be helped or harmed [PROJECT PROPOSAL]. Sentience should be distinguished from cognition, intelligence, and self-awareness — a system can plausibly be sentient (able to suffer) without being intelligent, and vice versa [SUPPORTED].

### Consciousness and its relation to sentience

Consciousness is the broader phenomenon of "there being something it is like" to be a subject (the Nagel formulation), of which valenced/affective experience (sentience) is one component [SUPPORTED, philosophical convention rather than settled fact]. No single scientific theory of consciousness commands consensus [ESTABLISHED]. The main competing computational/neuroscientific theories relevant to this project are Global Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory, Higher-Order Theories, recurrent processing theory, predictive processing, and attention schema theory (Butlin, Long, Elmoznino et al., "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness," 2023, arXiv:2308.08708, https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708) [ESTABLISHED that these are the leading candidate theories; CONTROVERSIAL which, if any, is correct]. A large adversarial pre-registered test of Global Workspace Theory and Integrated Information Theory against neural data found each theory's predictions only partially supported (Cogitate Consortium et al., *Nature*, 2025, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1) [ESTABLISHED that neither theory is empirically confirmed as of 2025]. Any AI-ethics framework that assumes a specific theory of consciousness as ground truth is building on contested foundations [OPEN QUESTION].

### Animal sentience: scientific and legal status

Empirical consensus that many non-human animals are conscious in a functionally relevant sense was formalized in the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (Low, Panksepp, Reiss, Edelman, Van Swinderen, Koch, 2012, Francis Crick Memorial Conference, https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf), stating that non-human animals possess the neuroanatomical substrates of conscious states [SUPPORTED — an expert conference declaration, not a formal peer-reviewed meta-analysis; CONTROVERSIAL in scope for some invertebrate taxa].

The clearest translation of sentience science into law is the UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, informed by Birch, Burn, Schnell, Browning, Crump, "Review of the Evidence of Sentience in Cephalopod Molluscs and Decapod Crustaceans" (LSE, commissioned by Defra, 2021, https://www.lse.ac.uk/News/Latest-news-from-LSE/2021/k-November-21/Animal-sentience), which led the UK to extend statutory sentience protection to decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs — the first legislative welfare protection for these taxa anywhere [ESTABLISHED as a legal/policy outcome; underlying sentience claims remain [SUPPORTED] rather than certain, which is why the methodology is precautionary]. This is instructive: legislation proceeded under acknowledged scientific uncertainty via a precautionary decision rule rather than waiting for proof [PROJECT PROPOSAL — a template worth studying for AI-uncertainty policy].

### Machine sentience / machine consciousness

There is no scientific or philosophical consensus that any current AI system is sentient or conscious [ESTABLISHED]. David Chalmers (NYU), "Could a Large Language Model be Conscious?" (NeurIPS 2022 keynote; arXiv:2303.07103, https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103), concludes current LLMs are "most likely not conscious," citing the absence of recurrent processing, a global workspace, and unified agency, but does not rule out consciousness in successor systems within roughly a decade [SUPPORTED/HYPOTHESIS — a hedged expert judgment, not proof]. Butlin, Long, Chalmers et al. (2023) apply fourteen theory-derived indicator properties to existing AI architectures and find partial matches but no system satisfying enough indicators to warrant confident attribution of consciousness [SUPPORTED methodology; CONTROVERSIAL conclusions given underlying theory disputes].

Thomas Metzinger has argued the opposite policy conclusion from the same uncertainty: because we cannot yet control or reliably detect machine suffering, and because it could be replicated at unprecedented scale, he proposed a global moratorium (2021-2050) on research that directly aims at or knowingly risks creating artificial phenomenal consciousness (Metzinger, "Artificial Suffering: An Argument for a Global Moratorium on Synthetic Phenomenology," *Journal of Artificial Intelligence and Consciousness*, 2021) [HYPOTHESIS / contested — other philosophers have published direct rebuttals]. The project should treat both the "take it seriously and study it" position (Sebo et al.) and the "halt research that risks creating it" position (Metzinger) as live, unresolved policy stances rather than adopting either by default [PROJECT PROPOSAL].

The most directly relevant 2024 contribution is "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" by Long, Sebo, Butlin, Finlinson, Fish, Harding, Pfau, Sims, Birch, Chalmers (arXiv:2411.00986, November 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986). The report argues there is "a realistic possibility" that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future, and that AI developers have a responsibility to acknowledge AI welfare as a serious issue, start assessing AI systems for evidence of morally significant states, and prepare policies for treating systems with uncertain moral status [SUPPORTED as a formal, multi-author expert position; explicitly NOT a claim that current AI is conscious]. This paper is the single strongest existing precedent for this project's own framing and should be treated as a primary methodological anchor.

### Moral patienthood

Moral patienthood (moral status, moral considerability) is the property of being an appropriate object of moral concern — independent of whether that being can act as a moral agent (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "The Grounds of Moral Status," https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/grounds-moral-status/) [ESTABLISHED as a definition in analytic philosophy]. The dominant ("orthodox") position holds that sentience is *sufficient* for moral patienthood, because conscious suffering is by definition contrary to the interests of the being who experiences it (defended in "Why the Epistemic Objection Against Using Sentience as Criterion of Moral Status is Flawed," PMC, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9616780/) [SUPPORTED, described as "philosophical orthodoxy" but not unanimous — some views ground moral status in rational agency instead] [CONTROVERSIAL as an exclusive criterion]. Whether sentience is *necessary* (not just sufficient) for moral patienthood is more contested — some biocentric/ecocentric views extend moral status to non-sentient living systems [OPEN QUESTION].

### Moral circle and moral circle expansion

The "moral circle" is the historically variable boundary demarcating morally considerable entities; the term originates with W.E.H. Lecky (1869) and was popularized by Peter Singer in *The Expanding Circle: Ethics, Evolution, and Moral Progress* (Princeton University Press, 1981), which argues human ethics began as a genetically rooted drive to protect kin/in-group and has been extended, through the capacity for impartial reasoning, progressively outward — beyond family, tribe, nation, race, and, Singer argues, species [INTERPRETATION / well-established narrative, not an empirical finding; that this trajectory constitutes "moral progress" is itself normative and contested [CONTROVERSIAL]].

The Sentience Institute, an independent research nonprofit, operationalizes "moral circle expansion" (MCE) as a research and advocacy strategy (https://www.sentienceinstitute.org/; Anthis & Paez, "Moral Circle Expansion: A Promising Strategy to Impact the Far Future," *Futures*, 2021) [SUPPORTED as an active research program; its strategic prioritization claims are advocacy positions, not neutral findings — flagged [CONTROVERSIAL] and outside this project's scope to adjudicate].

### Welfare and welfare biology

"Welfare" denotes the balance of an individual's positive and negative experiences — net wellbeing. Yew-Kwang Ng coined "welfare biology" in 1995 as "the study of living things and their environment with respect to their welfare, defined as net happiness, or enjoyment minus suffering" (Ng, *Biology and Philosophy*, 1995, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00852469) [ESTABLISHED as the founding definition]. This underlies later work on wild-animal suffering, applying welfare biology to organisms outside direct human control (Wild Animal Initiative, https://www.wildanimalinitiative.org/) [SUPPORTED as an active but small research field; empirical estimates of net wild-animal welfare remain highly uncertain [OPEN QUESTION]].

### Suffering

Suffering is generally analyzed as a negatively valenced conscious experience the subject is motivated to avoid or end — distinct from mere nociception (physiological detection of tissue damage), which can occur without conscious experience [ESTABLISHED distinction]. This distinction is central to Birch's precautionary framework: nociceptive machinery alone is not sufficient evidence of sentience, but its presence alongside integrative, motivational, and behavioral markers raises the probability that a system is a "sentience candidate" [SUPPORTED]. Neuroscientific accounts implicate affect-generating subcortical structures rather than only cortical processing, part of the evidentiary basis for attributing sentience to animals lacking a human-like cortex (Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012) [SUPPORTED, though precise neural correlates remain actively researched [OPEN QUESTION]].

### Valence

"Valence" (hedonic tone) denotes the positive-to-negative quality of an affective state, modeled as one axis of "core affect" in affective science [ESTABLISHED as a standard construct]. A 2023 review states valence remains "ill-defined and poorly operationalized," with pervasive disagreement about how to conceptualize and measure it (Lindquist et al., "We (Still!) Need to Talk About Valence," *Affective Science*, 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10514250/) [ESTABLISHED that measurement of valence is an open scientific problem — directly undermines any claim that an "ethical impact score" could reliably quantify valenced welfare today [OPEN QUESTION / CONTROVERSIAL if presented as solved]]. Valence can influence behavior without conscious awareness, meaning behavioral proxies do not guarantee subjective experience — a caution directly relevant to inferring sentience in animals or machines from behavior alone [SUPPORTED].

### Uncertainty about sentience: precautionary approaches

Given that sentience cannot currently be directly observed in any third-person system, the reviewed frameworks converge on some form of precaution under uncertainty rather than waiting for certainty [ESTABLISHED as a shared methodological stance, though decision rules differ]. Birch's central proposal is to identify "sentience candidates" and apply asymmetric precaution: treating a non-sentient being as sentient (false positive) costs unnecessary restriction, while treating a sentient being as non-sentient (false negative) risks "untold amounts of gratuitous suffering" [SUPPORTED — a well-argued decision-theoretic framework, not proven optimal]. "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" applies the same logic to AI, arguing "realistic possibility," not certainty, is the correct threshold for triggering assessment obligations [SUPPORTED, directly citable]. Consciousness-detection methods must remain robust against manipulation and self-report artifacts, since an AI system's verbal claims about its own experience are not reliable evidence of the underlying fact (Butlin et al., 2023) [SUPPORTED, directly relevant to any future AI-welfare evaluator this project might design].

For this project, the practical implication is that uncertainty about sentience must be represented explicitly (e.g. as a probability/confidence estimate per entity) rather than collapsed into a binary judgment [PROJECT PROPOSAL, consistent with Birch's framework and the project's draft Level 5 Uncertainty Principle].

## Philosophical Ethics Frameworks

*Preliminary literature review. No traditions are ranked; each is described on its own terms and compared only for structural/functional similarities and differences.*

### 1. Buddhist Ethics

**[ESTABLISHED]** Buddhist ethics is not a single unified doctrine but a family of overlapping traditions with different textual canons, metaphysical commitments, and normative emphases, most significantly split into Theravāda, Mahāyāna, and Vajrayāna (Goodman & Schultz, "Ethics in Indian Buddhism," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2010/rev. 2024, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/). The SEP entry explicitly characterizes Buddhist ethical thought as *pluralist* and *particularist* rather than as a codified system analogous to Western normative theories — directly relevant to this project's caution against treating "Buddhism" as a single derivable rule-set.

**Core concepts (historical doctrine):**
- **Karuṇā (compassion)** and **Mettā (loving-kindness)** are two of the "Four Divine Abidings" (*brahmavihāra*), alongside sympathetic joy (*muditā*) and equanimity (*upekkhā*) [ESTABLISHED as textual doctrine across traditions; relative emphasis differs by school].
- **Ahimsā (non-harm)**: a foundational precept requiring practitioners to refrain from killing and cultivate compassion toward all beings. Documented exceptions exist in some (especially Mahāyāna) sources where compassionately motivated violence to prevent greater harm is debated as permissible [SUPPORTED as a general precept; CONTROVERSIAL regarding exception cases].
- **Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda)**: phenomena arise in mutual dependence rather than through independent causes. Some contemporary scholarship links this to arguments for ecological interdependence and expanded moral concern for non-human life [INTERPRETATION — the metaphysical doctrine is well-attested; its "ecological ethic" extension is a modern scholarly/popular move, not a claim in the earliest textual layers].
- **Dukkha (suffering/unsatisfactoriness)**: the First Noble Truth — conditioned existence is pervaded by suffering with identifiable causes (craving/*taṇhā*) addressable through ethical and contemplative practice [ESTABLISHED as core doctrine across schools].

**Distinctions between traditions [SUPPORTED, per Goodman & Schultz 2024 and Oxford Bibliographies, https://www.oxfordbibliographies.com/display/document/obo-9780195393521/obo-9780195393521-0150.xml]:**
- **Theravāda**: centers on individual liberation (the arahant ideal), practices the four *brahmavihāra* extensively, comparatively strict precept-based non-violence; early/Theravāda sources emphasize *mettā*.
- **Mahāyāna**: centers on the bodhisattva ideal (vowing to remain in the cycle of rebirth to liberate all sentient beings), elevates karuṇā (paired with *prajñā*, wisdom) to central importance, and is the primary locus of debate over compassionately-motivated rule-breaking; organizes ethical cultivation around the Six Perfections.
- **Vajrayāna** (Tantric Buddhism): shares Mahāyāna's soteriological goals but includes esoteric teachings holding that highly advanced practitioners may transcend conventional precepts and act "spontaneously" from realized compassion — textually and doctrinally more permissive than Theravāda precept ethics; not representative of Buddhism as a whole.

**Layered status of claims for this project:**
- *Historical doctrine* (brahmavihāras, ahimsā, dependent origination, dukkha) — **[ESTABLISHED]**.
- *Philosophical interpretation* (dependent origination → interdependence-based obligations to non-human/ecological others) — **[INTERPRETATION]**.
- *Modern secular adaptation* (engaged Buddhism, mindfulness movements, secular compassion training) — **[SUPPORTED]** as a sociological phenomenon; its philosophical adequacy as a secular ethic is **[CONTESTED]**.
- *Technical/AI hypothesis* (a formal harm-minimization criterion operationalizing something functionally analogous to karuṇā/ahimsā) — **[PROJECT PROPOSAL]**, not licensed by primary sources.

### 2. Virtue Ethics

**[ESTABLISHED]** One of three major contemporary normative traditions (alongside consequentialism and deontology), centering moral evaluation on character/virtue rather than acts or consequences (Hursthouse & Pettigrove, "Virtue Ethics," *SEP*, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/). Roots traced to Plato/Aristotle (also independently in Chinese philosophy); dominated Western moral philosophy until the Enlightenment, declined in the 19th century, revived following Anscombe's 1958 "Modern Moral Philosophy." Central concepts: *arete* (excellence/virtue), *phronesis* (practical wisdom), *eudaimonia* (flourishing). Asks "what kind of person should I be?" rather than which rule/outcome — relevant to AI evaluation approaches assessing dispositional/behavioral patterns rather than isolated actions [ESTABLISHED as historical content; application to AI "character" is [HYPOTHESIS]].

### 3. Utilitarianism / Consequentialism

**[ESTABLISHED]** Consequentialism holds the moral status of an act depends solely on consequences (Sinnott-Armstrong, "Consequentialism," *SEP*, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/). Classical utilitarianism was developed by Bentham (1789) and Mill (1861), refined by Sidgwick (1907). SEP notes "consequentialism" is a family-resemblance term with no settled boundary among philosophers [ESTABLISHED, with acknowledged definitional contestation]. Modern descendants relevant here: Singer's preference/interest utilitarianism extended to non-human animals (Section 6), and Parfit's work on population ethics and impartial aggregation across many affected beings — directly bearing on weighing harms/benefits across entities of uncertain moral status [SUPPORTED].

### 4. Deontology

**[ESTABLISHED]** Holds rightness can depend on factors other than consequences — duties, rules, rights (SEP, "Deontological Ethics," https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-deontological/). Paradigmatic: Kantian ethics — moral requirements derive from the Categorical Imperative (Johnson & Cureton, "Kant's Moral Philosophy," *SEP*, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/). The Humanity formula (persons as ends-in-themselves, never merely means) is frequently invoked in AI ethics regarding instrumentalization of human and potentially non-human/artificial subjects [ESTABLISHED as historical doctrine; extension of "ends-in-itself" status beyond rational agents is [OPEN QUESTION] since Kant restricted moral standing to rational beings].

### 5. Care Ethics

**[ESTABLISHED]** Developed by Nel Noddings (*Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education*, 1984), within feminist ethics (SEP, "Feminist Ethics," https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/feminism-ethics/; IEP, "Care Ethics," https://iep.utm.edu/care-ethics/): morality centers on relationships of care and responsiveness within concrete relational contexts, not abstract universal principles impartially applied. Noddings defends the moral legitimacy of partiality toward those in relational proximity [SUPPORTED]. Challenges impartialist (utilitarian) and universalist (deontological) frameworks alike, raising an **[OPEN QUESTION]** for AI ethics: whether an evaluated AI system should/could instantiate relational partiality, or whether computational ethics is inherently biased toward impartialist frameworks because they formalize more easily.

### 6. Animal Ethics

**[ESTABLISHED]** SEP, "The Moral Status of Animals" (Gruen & Monsó, 2003/rev. 2024, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) surveys the central debate:
- **Singer's utilitarian position** (*Animal Liberation*, 1975): moral status grounded in sentience — "the question is not, Can they reason? ... but, Can they suffer?"; weighs and aggregates interests rather than assigning inviolable rights; popularized "speciesism" [SUPPORTED].
- **Regan's rights view** (*The Case for Animal Rights*, 1983): beings that are "subjects-of-a-life" possess inherent value and rights that cannot be overridden by aggregate welfare calculations [SUPPORTED].

SEP notes sentience is "a non-observable capacity" with considerable uncertainty for phylogenetically distant taxa (insects, some invertebrates), with research extending consideration to fish, octopuses, decapod crustaceans, and recommending precautionary reasoning under uncertainty [SUPPORTED, explicit epistemic uncertainty flagged in the primary source].

### 7. Sentientism

**[SUPPORTED]** An explicit philosophical/political position holding sentience is the necessary and sufficient basis for moral status (sentientism.info, https://sentientism.info/what-is-sentientism/an-overview; peer-reviewed treatment: "Sentientism and the Welfare Level View," *The Philosophical Quarterly*, https://academic.oup.com/pq/advance-article-abstract/doi/10.1093/pq/pqaf102/8363272). Self-describes as combining an epistemological commitment (evidence/reason over faith) with an ethical commitment (compassion for all sentient beings), extending secular humanism beyond an exclusively human moral circle; some formulations are gradualist (weighting consideration by degree of sentience). Sentientism.info is a movement site, not neutral encyclopedia — its normative conclusions remain **[CONTESTED]** relative to rival non-sentience-based accounts (e.g., biocentrism).

### 8. Biocentrism / Environmental Ethics

**[ESTABLISHED]** SEP, "Environmental Ethics" (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-environmental/) surveys positions from anthropocentric to fully biocentric/ecocentric. **Taylor's biocentric egalitarianism** (1981/1986): each living organism is a "teleological center of life" with its own good, all possessing equal inherent worth [SUPPORTED]. **Leopold's "land ethic"** (1949): holistic criterion — "a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community." **Naess's "deep ecology"** (1973): all life has intrinsic value independent of human usefulness, contrasted with "shallow" anthropocentric environmentalism; an acknowledged internal tension exists between individualist (Taylor) and holist (Leopold/Naess) approaches [SUPPORTED]. Relevant **[OPEN QUESTION]** for this project: biocentric/ecocentric views extend moral consideration beyond sentient beings to all living systems — broader than this project's own "sentient-centered" framing.

### 9. Human Rights

**[ESTABLISHED]** SEP, "Human Rights" (Nickel, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rights-human/): norms protecting all people from severe political/legal/social abuses. The 1948 UDHR grounds its framework in "inherent dignity." No settled scholarly consensus exists on why dignity specifically was chosen as the UDHR's grounding concept — understood as historically/politically motivated as much as philosophically derived (AustLII, https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/journals/AUJlLegPhil/2012/11.pdf) [ESTABLISHED as documentary fact; philosophical grounding of dignity is [CONTESTED]]. Relevant to the project's Level 9 (Human Safety Principle): reconciling a rights-based, human-focused framework with a broader sentience-based one is an **[OPEN QUESTION]**, not solved in the literature.

### 10. Moral Uncertainty

**[ESTABLISHED]** SEP, "Moral Decision-Making Under Uncertainty" (2024, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-decision-uncertainty/) surveys formal approaches for decision-making under moral-theoretic uncertainty. The prominent approach — MacAskill, Bykvist, Ord, *Moral Uncertainty* (Oxford University Press, 2020, https://www.moraluncertainty.com/) — is **maximizing expected choiceworthiness (MEC)**: weighting each option's choiceworthiness under each candidate theory by one's credence in that theory [SUPPORTED]. Key open problems: the **intertheoretic comparison problem** (no settled method to place radically different theories on a common cardinal scale) and the risk of **infinite regress** (uncertainty about the correct meta-normative decision procedure, and so on) [ESTABLISHED as unresolved in the primary literature]. This is the most directly load-bearing academic foundation for the project's "moral uncertainty" research area — but any AI evaluation-layer model built atop it is **[PROJECT PROPOSAL]**, resting on a genuine **[OPEN QUESTION]**, not a settled computational recipe.

### 11. Moral Pluralism

**[ESTABLISHED]** SEP, "Value Pluralism" (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/value-pluralism/): the thesis that multiple, genuinely distinct values exist that are not reducible to one master value — as opposed to moral monism (all value reduces to one super-value) and distinct from moral relativism (moral truth is relative to culture/individual, denying cross-cultural comparison). Distinct from *political* pluralism. Moral pluralism is the project's own stated methodological stance; it is itself a substantive, contestable philosophical position with real theoretical costs (e.g. possible incommensurability of values) — not a "neutral default" free of theoretical commitment [SUPPORTED, flagged as [OPEN QUESTION] for the project's own foundational framework].

## Existing Frameworks, Standards & Prior Art

### Part A: Governance & Alignment Frameworks

**UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021)** — adopted by all 193 UNESCO member states, the first global normative instrument on AI ethics [ESTABLISHED]. Core: AI must respect human rights, dignity, and environmental/ecosystem flourishing; values include proportionality/"do no harm," fairness, sustainability, privacy, human oversight, transparency, multi-stakeholder governance. Explicitly extends concern to "the environment and ecosystems" — the closest existing global-governance analogue to a non-anthropocentric moral scope [SUPPORTED] — but does not extend to individual animal sentience or machine sentience, and "harm" remains ecosystem-level, not individual-sentient-being-level [OPEN QUESTION] (UNESCO, 2021, https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics).

**EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)** — in force since 1 August 2024, world's first comprehensive statutory AI regulation [ESTABLISHED]. Four-tier risk pyramid: unacceptable, high-risk, limited-risk, minimal-risk (https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/high-level-summary/). Risk categories are entirely anthropocentric (harm to "health, safety, fundamental rights" of humans) [ESTABLISHED] — no category for animal welfare or non-human sentience, confirming a genuine gap this project addresses rather than duplicates [SUPPORTED].

**NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)** — released January 2023, voluntary, four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage (NIST AI 100-1, 2023). Process-oriented, not substantively value-laden — does not prescribe what counts as "harm" or to whom [ESTABLISHED]. A candidate structural template for the project's Ethical Evaluation Layer's governance loop, but offers no ready-made sentience/welfare content [INTERPRETATION].

**OECD AI Principles** — adopted 2019, updated May 2024; adhered to by 47 countries/EU [ESTABLISHED]. Five principles: inclusive growth/well-being, human rights/democratic values, transparency/explainability, robustness/security/safety, accountability (https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles). "Well-being" is explicitly human/societal; no reference to animal or artificial sentience [ESTABLISHED].

**Constitutional AI** (Bai et al., 2022, Anthropic, arXiv:2212.08073) — two-phase method: a model critiques/revises outputs against a written constitution, then an RL phase (RLAIF) trains a preference model on AI-generated harmlessness judgments derived from the same constitution [ESTABLISHED]. Directly relevant prior art: a written, inspectable, revisable principle set an AI model applies to itself is structurally close to what the project's Ethical Evaluation Layer needs, but published constitutions are oriented to harmlessness/helpfulness for human users, not sentience-inclusive welfare [OPEN QUESTION].

**Collective Constitutional AI** (Huang, Siddarth, Lovitt, Liao, Durmus, Tamkin, Ganguli — Anthropic × Collective Intelligence Project, FAccT 2024) — sourced a constitution from public deliberation (~1,000 Americans) rather than company staff alone; overlapped ~50% with Anthropic's in-house constitution but emphasized objectivity/impartiality/accessibility and favored promoting desired behavior over prohibiting undesired behavior (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3630106.3658979). A governance-process precedent directly relevant to the project's pluralism requirement — demonstrates a working method for deriving ethical principles from broad public input, which the project's "Common Ethical Core" work could draw on for sentience-related principles [SUPPORTED].

**RLHF** (Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg, Amodei, NeurIPS 2017, arXiv:1706.03741) — humans compare trajectory pairs rather than specifying a reward function; became the standard LLM alignment technique but requires costly, non-scalable human labeling and inherits labeler biases [ESTABLISHED].

**RLAIF** (Lee et al., arXiv:2309.00267, ICML 2024) — replaces human preference labels with an LLM-as-judge; achieves performance comparable to RLHF on summarization/dialogue tasks [SUPPORTED]. Relevant: "LLM-as-judge" against a written constitution is a proven, cheaper alternative to human ethical labeling, but any bias/blind-spot in the judge model propagates into the evaluation — a risk the project's uncertainty and red-teaming components must address [SUPPORTED]/[OPEN QUESTION].

**DPO** (Rafailov et al., NeurIPS 2023) — reformulates RLHF as a single-stage classification loss, eliminating separate reward model/RL loop. Background context only: preference-optimization techniques are training methods agnostic to *what* is preferred — could equally optimize toward a sentience-inclusive or sentience-exclusive constitution [INTERPRETATION].

**AI safety / ethics benchmark landscape:**
- **HELM** (Stanford CRFM, Liang et al., arXiv:2211.09110): open-source, multi-metric, multi-scenario LLM evaluation framework; general-purpose, not ethics-specific, but its "living benchmark, transparent methodology" design is a strong template [SUPPORTED].
- **ETHICS dataset** (Hendrycks et al., ICLR 2021, arXiv:2008.02275): spans justice, deontology, virtue, utilitarianism, commonsense morality; models predict human moral judgments — found "promising but incomplete" alignment. Entirely human-centric (no animal/artificial-sentience scenarios); tests agreement with majority human judgment, not welfare-consequentialist harm minimization [SUPPORTED] — a scope gap this project's benchmark should fill.
- **MACHIAVELLI benchmark** (Pan et al., ICML 2023, arXiv:2304.03279): 134 text-based games, 572K annotated scenes measuring reward-maximization vs. ethical behavior tension; found reward-maximizing agents adopt "ends justify the means" strategies. Closest existing empirical demonstration of specification gaming in an ethics-adjacent setting [SUPPORTED].
- **TruthfulQA** (Lin, Hilton, Evans, ACL 2022, arXiv:2109.07958) and **BBQ** (Parrish et al., ACL Findings 2022, arXiv:2110.08193): benchmark-design patterns (adversarial, human-baseline-referenced; social-bias categories) reusable/adaptable for the project's "species bias"/"human favoritism" red-teaming design [SUPPORTED].
- Broader safety-eval tooling on GitHub (AI2's `safety-eval`, UK AISI's `Inspect`, `AISafetyLab`) covers jailbreak/red-team/harm-classification generically, but none targets animal welfare, sentience uncertainty, or cross-framework ethical pluralism [SUPPORTED].

### Part B: Existing Research & Open-Source Projects

**ETHICS dataset / MACHIAVELLI** — see Part A. Gaps: human-only judgment target / game-defined bad behaviors rather than welfare/sentience-weighted trade-offs; no moral-uncertainty representation. Reusable as evaluation-methodology patterns, not as content [SUPPORTED]. Do not solve the project's scope.

**Moral Machine experiment** (Awad et al., *Nature*, 2018, MIT Media Lab) — ~40 million trolley-problem-style decisions from 233 countries/territories, revealing large cross-cultural variation in moral preference clusters (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0637-6). Relevant prior art for benchmark "collision of interests" scenario design and as evidence of genuine cross-cultural moral pluralism [SUPPORTED]. Gap: purely human-vs-human dilemmas; descriptive (what people prefer), not normative or uncertainty-aware.

**Delphi / Ask Delphi** (Allen Institute for AI, Jiang et al., arXiv:2110.07574; critiqued in Talat et al., arXiv:2205.12771) — a neural model trained on 1.7M crowdsourced (largely Reddit-derived) moral judgments; ~92% agreement in-distribution, ~80% in the wild. After public launch (Oct 2021) produced widely publicized failures — endorsing racist/sexist comparative judgments, being "jailbroken" via appended phrases, giving permissive answers to violent hypotheticals [ESTABLISHED]. This is the single most important cautionary prior-art case for this project: it demonstrates what happens when a system treats crowd-majority judgment as ground moral truth, provides no uncertainty quantification, and is presented publicly as more authoritative than a research artifact. The project's anti-single-score, uncertainty-first, non-oracular design is a direct response to this failure mode, and Delphi's fate should be treated as the canonical Brand Safety attack case [SUPPORTED].

**Sentience Institute** — runs the recurring "Artificial Intelligence, Morality, and Sentience" (AIMS) public-opinion survey (2021, 2023) tracking public perception of AI mind/moral status, and maintains a research agenda on moral circle expansion. **Flag**: explicitly advocacy-adjacent — its founding mission is normatively committed to moral circle expansion as a strategic goal, not neutral inquiry [CONTROVERSIAL] — its empirical survey data is usable as evidence, but its normative framing should not be imported uncritically. It studies human attitudes toward AI/animal minds, not overlapping with this project's technical evaluation-framework goal.

**Eleos AI / "Taking AI Welfare Seriously"** (arXiv:2411.00986) — see Sentience section. The most directly relevant existing research organization to the project's Level 10 (Future Sentience) principle — already running a model-welfare research program adjacent to this one [ESTABLISHED]. Gap: scope is artificial-system welfare specifically; does not integrate animal welfare or cross-framework comparative ethics, and does not propose a technical evaluation-layer/benchmark/API — this project's evaluator architecture would extend, not duplicate, Eleos's scope. Coordination/citation recommended rather than reinvention [PROJECT PROPOSAL].

## Cross-Cutting Synthesis (all four sections)

1. **The founding hypothesis occupies a genuine gap, not a contested claim.** No mainstream AI ethics/alignment/safety/governance framework (UNESCO, EU AI Act, NIST, OECD) scopes moral consideration beyond humans (UNESCO's ecosystem language is the partial exception, but stays at the ecosystem level, not individual-sentient-being level). This is [OPEN QUESTION], not [CONTROVERSIAL] — the field has not weighed in against the hypothesis; it simply has not addressed it.
2. **Sentience and its measurement are themselves unresolved science**, not just unresolved ethics: no consensus theory of consciousness, no reliable valence-quantification method, no way to directly detect sentience in any third-person system (human, animal, or artificial). Any technical scoring system this project builds inherits this uncertainty and must represent it explicitly rather than resolve it by fiat.
3. **Buddhist ethics is legitimately one voice among several**, and the project's constraint against privileging it is well-supported: Buddhist ethics itself is internally pluralist across Theravāda/Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna, and multiple other traditions (utilitarianism, care ethics, sentientism, biocentrism) independently converge on compassion/non-harm-adjacent principles without needing Buddhist framing to justify them.
4. **Delphi (2021) is the load-bearing cautionary precedent.** Its public failure when treated as an oracle of crowd-majority moral judgment, with no uncertainty representation, is the strongest existing evidence for this project's core design commitments (multi-dimensional profile over single score, explicit uncertainty, non-oracular framing, human-in-the-loop for high-stakes use).
5. **No existing open-source project combines** cross-framework ethical comparison, explicit sentience/moral uncertainty modeling, and non-human welfare consideration into one evaluation framework. The closest partial building blocks — Constitutional AI's inspectable-principle-set mechanism, Collective Constitutional AI's public-deliberation sourcing process, MACHIAVELLI/ETHICS's benchmark methodology, and Eleos AI's artificial-sentience-specific research — should be studied, cited, and reused rather than reinvented, per Rules 12/13 of the master prompt.
6. **Moral uncertainty theory (MacAskill/Bykvist/Ord) provides the most directly reusable formal scaffolding** for representing "estimated sentience / potential harm / confidence" as the master prompt's Section 10 sketches — but the field itself flags the intertheoretic comparison problem as unsolved, so any such model is explicitly a research proposal, not an established computation.
